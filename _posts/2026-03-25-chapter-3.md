They saddled the horses under the pale morning light. Each mount was fitted with special broad shoes which were flat, ridged metal that allowed them to grip the shifting dunes without sinking. Robert checked every strap and buckle twice before handing over the reins.

Only Julie and James, aside from Robert, moved with the quiet confidence of seasoned riders. Shively swung up behind Julie, his knees pressing awkwardly against the saddle’s edge, but his eyes were sharp, scanning the horizon. Beverley climbed onto the back of Robert’s horse without a word, her hands light on the edges of the saddle, careful not to cling too tightly. The fourth horse was used to carry the supplies with James handling the rope tie.

The wind shifted, carrying with it the faint tang of rust. The horizon becoming more red as day moved on. 

Shively broke the silence. “If we’re lucky, we won’t run into Crawlers or Feeders before Nirrowen. But…” He tapped his temple. “I’ve read enough to know what to watch for.”

Robert glanced back at him. “Then tell them.”

“The Crawlers came after the sky started raining ash and rust,” Shively began. “Think of giant insects, only… their shells are iron and their legs can crush bone. They don’t usually eat people right away. They prefer greenery, but…” his voice lowered, “…in a world this barren, they’ll bite anything if they’re hungry.”

Beverley’s eyes stayed fixed on the shifting dunes ahead, but she listened.

“The Feeders,” Shively continued, “are worse. Animals that fused with mechanical parts. Carnivores. They do feed on people. Some cities hire mercenaries to keep their numbers down. Luckily, they breed slow because it takes a very specific male and female, each with the right mix of biology and machinery, for them to reproduce.”

“Good,” Robert said flatly. “That means we’ll only be fighting the hungry ones.”

James gave a dry chuckle. “Hungry is worse than many.”

The horses pressed on, their metal-fitted hooves crunching over hardened sand. Overhead, the sun climbed higher, and in the far distance, something small moved across the dunes.

Julie leaned back slightly toward Shively. “You said if we’re lucky.”

“Yeah,” Shively muttered, “but the desert’s not really known for giving out luck.”

They crested a low dune and froze.

Below them, an oasis shimmered in the heat, as if to state that this desert does give out good luck. But this miracle had already drawn its predators. A nest of Crawlers swarmed the grassland surrounding the oasis, their segmented iron shells clicking against one another as they tore up thick blades of grass and shredded low shrubs.

James raised a hand, signaling the group to halt. He knew this type very well. Some were ground-bound feeders, but among them would be the fliers. And the fliers were the real danger. If one spotted prey and returned to the nest, the entire swarm would descend like a storm.

“Quiet,” he mouthed.

The group eased their horses into a slow, deliberate walk, skirting the edge of the dune’s slope. The Crawlers were so intent on feeding that they didn’t notice the riders passing. Their metal mandibles grinding, hydraulic limbs flexing as they stripped the greenery bare.

Julie’s grip on her reins tightened. She remembered too vividly the last time she had been this close. How one had broken from the herd and lunged at her. She’d barely managed to drive her spear into its electrical center, cutting its power in a sharp, blinding jolt.

Shively watched the swarm with almost academic fascination. His eyes traced the strange variety among them. Some nearly all mechanical, their organic parts reduced to withered scraps of muscle; others still mostly flesh, with only glints of rusted metal in their limbs or along their spines. He noted how they gathered in tiers, larger units claiming the best feeding spots while smaller ones waited their turn, skittering impatiently along the outskirts.

When they cleared the far edge of the grassland, the group finally exhaled as one. The oasis faded behind them, but the sound of the Crawlers’ grinding mandibles lingered in their ears.

“Too close,” Robert murmured.

“Closer than I’d like to get again,” Julie admitted.

“Noted,” Shively said softly, already committing every detail to memory.

They spotted it on the horizon first, a hulking shape trotting toward them through the sand. As it drew closer, the form became clear: a Feeder, its silhouette unmistakable.

It mimicked the shape of a wild dog, but at three times the size. Its face gleamed with exposed metal plating, eyes glowing faintly, while the rest of its body remained stubbornly organic. With lean muscle, coarse fur, and long legs built for speed.

Robert’s instincts kicked in instantly. He slid from the saddle, spear in hand, the length of rope coiled at his side. He’d killed hundreds of Crawlers and Feeders before. Here its attack first, think later. James mirrored the motion, a throwing knife already spinning between his fingers.

The Feeder slowed, ears pivoting toward them, golden eyes fixed and unblinking.

“Wait,” Shively hissed, raising a hand. “This one isn’t aggressive.”

Robert glanced over his shoulder, incredulous.

“I’ve read about a strain with just enough domesticated dog in them that they actually like people,” Shively went on.

Julie shot him a skeptical look. “Shiv, I have never heard of that in my life.”

Beverley didn’t say a word. She swung a leg over the horse and dropped to the ground, hand extended.

The Feeder’s massive paws pressed into the sand as it approached her cautious with nose twitching.

Robert stepped down too, keeping his spear ready. He wasn’t sure whether Beverley had some unspoken experience with these creatures, or if she simply had a way of pulling the gentlest side out of even the most dangerous things.

“Beverley,” he called, voice low. “Have you met one like this before?”

She shook her head without looking at him, still coaxing the dog closer with soft clicks of her tongue.

The Feeder sniffed her palm, then closed the gap entirely pressing its head into her hand like an obedient pet. For a moment, it was as though it had always belonged to people.

Robert frowned. “Strange he’s alone. They keep to packs.”

That’s when the sand behind them shifted.

A low, chittering hum rose over the still air, and the first shadows crept into view.

From over the next rise, a small herd of Crawlers spilled into the basin with eyes glowing, mandibles twitching, their mechanical legs hissing as they closed in.

The dog-Feeder’s ears flattened.

Robert lifted his spear again. “Well,” he muttered, “get ready.”

Weapons flashed in the sunlight as everyone sprang into motion. The Feeder-dog planted itself between Beverley and the incoming swarm, lips peeling back to reveal steel-tipped fangs. Normally, Feeders avoided Crawlers. Mechanical parts weren’t worth the trouble but this one lunged without hesitation, defending her like it had sworn an oath.

The first Crawler darted forward, its segmented legs a blur. Robert met it head-on, his spear driving clean through a vulnerable patch of exposed flesh near its thorax. Sparks spat from its frame before it crumpled in a twitching heap. He pivoted, finding another seam between armor plates, striking true again.

Beside him, James worked in perfect rhythm. His knife flashing, striking deep into the organic “hearts” at the center of each beast, his other hand already reaching for the next target.

Two Crawlers veered toward Beverley, but the Feeder leapt in an arc of muscle and metal, colliding midair. The impact rang like struck iron. In seconds, two mechanical husks lay twitching in the sand.

Julie’s hooks whirled through the air, chain links singing. A Crawler with buzzing wings banked toward her; she snagged it mid-flight, yanked hard, and slammed it into the earth with a sound like shattering glass.

Shively moved like water slipping between the Crawlers’ strikes, dropping low to sweep their legs, then slamming his palms into their cores. The blows weren’t always fatal, but they stunned his targets long enough for Robert or James to finish them off. Julie caught sight of his speed and almost lost focus. He moved like a trained warrior, but she knew it was furthest form the truth.

Beverley stayed just behind the line; eyes locked not on the Crawlers but on the men ahead of her. Robert’s precise, driving strikes, James’ quick, lethal jabs. They fought like they had been trained by the same hand, one dismantling flesh, the other cutting power.

By the time the last Crawler screeched and fell still, the only movement in the clearing was the slow prowl of the Feeder, circling Beverley, scanning for any more threats.

Robert planted the butt of his spear in the sand. “Seven,” he said between breaths. “That’s… not bad.”

The Feeder returned to Beverley’s side, pressing against her hip. She laid a hand on its head.

They pressed on toward Nirrowen, the last city on their list, sand crunching under the special steel shoes of their horses. The Feeder-dog never strayed far from Beverley’s side, matching their pace with an effortless lope.

“What are you going to name him?” Robert asked, voice light but curious.

“Fang,” Beverley replied without hesitation.

Robert’s brows lifted. He looked the creature over. He had a gleaming metal jaw, lean organic frame, watchful eyes. Robert then gave a single approving nod. “Good boy, Fang.”

Julie tilted her head toward Shively. “So… did you actually read about this ‘special breed’ of Feeder, or were you just making it up, so we didn’t skewer him?”

Shively looked skyward as if flipping through the pages of his memory. “I think I did. But… it’s entirely possible it was more of a premonition than a fact.”

Julie snorted. “Figures.”

James rode ahead of the group, scanning the horizon. He slowed when the sand began to thin, giving way to craggy, wind-cut stone. Squinting, he spotted the jagged lip of a cliff not far ahead.

“Robby,” he called over his shoulder, “what’s the plan when we hit the cliff?”

Robert’s attention shifted from Beverley to James. A faint, knowing smile touched his face. “I’ll show you.”
