When the sun began to peek over the dunes, James was the first to stir. He brushed sand off his sleeves and stood quietly, taking in the hush of morning. The group lay bundled close, closer than the night before. Julie and Shively, now fully intertwined, looked like more than just teammates. James let out a soft chuckle. That confirmed it. They’d had a fling once, maybe still did. Either way, they meant something to each other.

A sudden rustle beside him, Beverley had risen silently, perhaps stirred by his movement. Without a word, she began folding the giant leaves and packing up what little they had.

Julie and Shively woke around the same time. 

“Alright,” James began, slinging his bag over his shoulder. “We’ve still got another day’s walk ahead of us. Should be there before nightfall, though. I suggest we take some of these large leaves, use them to cover our heads. Might help with the heat.”

Beverley nodded, already tucking one into her belt. “James, you’re quite the wise old man,” Julie teased. “Old? I’m forty-three. I’ve got at least twenty years before you can call me that.” Shively laughed. “James, you don’t look a day over thirty. Jewels just thinks anyone with a backache is elderly.”

Julie shot him a playful glare, then grinned. “Only if they complain about it.”

With leaves overhead and sand crunching beneath their boots, the group pressed forward. They made better time than expected, and by late afternoon, the stone spires of Ovestra rose like jagged monuments against the red-gold sky. Just outside the gates, they found a shaded ridge where they could rest for the moment.

They were dusty, tired, and sun-kissed. After a brief moment of respite under the shade of stone ridges, the group moved through the great arched gates. The city was carved from the cliffside, its buildings sculpted from pale granite and burnished brick, bearing the ancient texture of a place that had weathered many storms and many flames.

James led the group with steady confidence, but inside, he was turning over possibilities. Where would someone like Robert be in a city like this? They searched the usual places first, public halls, training yards, even the bustling central marketplace but Robert Greaves was nowhere in sight. Then, like a drop of water into silence, Beverley spoke. Her voice was calm, but it carried.

“He works in fire prevention. That’s his unit. He helps with burns and structural containment during flare incidents.”

James turned to her, surprise flickering across his face. It wasn’t just the information, it was the certainty in her voice. “Ah,” he said, cocking an eyebrow. “Thanks, Beverley.”

She offered no explanation, no further words. But James made a mental note: there was more to Beverley than she let on. And she definitely knew Robert.

The group stood still for a moment, glancing around as people bustled past. No one had the faintest idea where the fire prevention unit was based.

Until Julie scanned the skyline with trained eyes. “You all really don’t have an eye for city planning, huh?” she muttered. “If I were putting fire response in a city like this, I’d put it in the center, equal distance from all districts. So no matter where the fire starts, they’re never too far.”

She pointed to a massive building directly in the heart of the city. Its sandstone exterior shimmered slightly in the heat, but the large brass initials affixed above the archway left no doubt: FP - Fire Prevention.

Shively whistled and grinned. “Wow, Jewels… you never cease to amaze me.”

Julie didn’t answer. She just smirked with a proud gleam in her eye and marched toward the building.

Beverley followed close behind, and James stood back for a second, watching.

When they all walked through the wide front doors of the fire prevention headquarters, the smell of burnt wood and sweat hit them first, followed by the hard stares of several broad-shouldered men in dark red uniforms.

One particularly scruffy man stepped forward, his voice rough like gravel. “What do you all want? You can’t just march in here like you’re on official business when you got no business here.”

Another man flanked him, arms crossed, his scowl deepening. “You’d better be gone before our head of command sees you wasting our time.”

James remained calm, analyzing their posture and tone like a soldier measuring threat levels. He spoke without hesitation. “He wouldn’t happen to be Robert Greaves, would he?”

The scruffy man raised a brow, jaw tightening. “Yeah. What’s it to you? He’s a busy man. If you’re here to waste his time, you’ve got another thing coming.”

Behind James, Beverley inched closer, her usually distant presence suddenly alert as if preparing for something inevitable.

“I have a letter,” James said. His hand went to his chest, tapping the folded parchment. “Maybe you’ve heard of the prophecy. Robert’s on the list. He has to come with us.”

The mention of the prophecy made the two men stiffen. Their bravado flickered, even as one of them pressed on, still wary. “Letter? That’s not proof. Anyone can walk in and claim they’ve got a letter… especially if they’re carrying out a hit.”

His eyes darted briefly to Julie, as though sizing her up as the one likely to do the dirty work. She stared back with her arms crossed, a look that said she dared him to try anything.

That’s when a calm voice broke through the tension.

“I’m on the list, am I?”

The men parted immediately, like the Red Sea before a storm. A tall figure entered the room, clean-cut, composed, bearing a quiet authority that demanded respect without raising his voice.

Robert Greaves.

His eyes scanned the group quickly, then landed squarely on Beverley. A spark of recognition passed between them. His gaze lingered, not romantic, but reverent, perhaps even haunted.

Beverley didn’t look away. She nodded once, silently confirming what they both already knew.

Robert took a slow breath. “Very well. I’ll pack my things.”

He turned to the two men. “Fission. Turic. Get them to the Stone Guard sleeping quarters. The rest of the unit is deployed, so they can make use of the beds, the showers, and whatever rations we’ve got stocked.”

Fission, the scruffy one, snapped to attention, visibly embarrassed by his earlier suspicion. “Yes, sir.”

Turic grunted. “We’ll see to it, sir.”

“And get them food,” Robert added as he began walking off. “Plenty. They look like they’ve crossed the entire continent already.”

Julie nudged Shively with a smirk. “That’s because we have.”

Julie and Beverley took the first round of showers, the layout offering little privacy beyond the frosted glass of the entryway. Shively stood guard just outside, arms folded and posture sharp, not out of distrust for Robert’s men, but out of habit, duty, and a need to feel useful.

It didn’t take long for that usefulness to be tested.

Fission came bumbling down the corridor with a half-empty sack of dried meat. “Thought the kitchen was….”

Shively cut him off with a cold glare and an outstretched arm. “Not this way. Rations are two doors down.”

Fission’s eyes widened. “Ah. Right. Thanks, friend.” He backed away quickly, muttering something about architectural flaws.

Inside the shower room, Beverley quietly disrobed, her white temple linen now dulled by the grit and sun, more beige than pure. She placed it gently on a hook and stepped beneath the nearest shower head. The water sputtered, then flowed, frigid, with only the faintest trace of warmth. Beverley didn’t flinch.

Julie stepped under the opposite shower, her muscles sighing under the spray. “How do you know him?” she asked, voice casual, but not careless.

Beverley didn’t answer right away. Her shoulders tensed slightly, betraying the stillness in her face.

“I don’t,” she said finally, the words clipped.

Julie chuckled softly, rinsing dust from her arms. “Look, we’re heading into something none of us might come back from. Building trust doesn’t sound like the worst idea.”

She turned to face Beverley, who stood rooted beneath the flow like a statue carved of marble and ash.

“I met him once,” Beverley said, her voice barely louder than the water. “But not formally. He doesn’t remember me. I wouldn’t expect him to.”

Julie raised an eyebrow. “You’re kidding, right? Did you see the way he looked at you? He was trying to remember. Like some part of him already knew.”

Beverley remained quiet, continuing her methodical cleansing. She didn’t argue, but she didn’t agree either.

Julie took the silence as her answer.

She closed her eyes, letting the cold rinse away the heat of the sun and the weight of the days behind them. The water was a luxury. With only one more name on the list, there might only be one more chance to feel something this normal.

The next round of showers belonged to Shively and James. They were in and out quickly, efficient, military-fast. No lingering, no chatting. Just rinse, scrub, dry.

Back in the sleeping quarters, James picked the cot closest to the entrance, simply a preference for first watch. Beverley quietly chose the cot furthest to the back, near a small window cracked open for airflow. It created a natural distance between her and the others, and no one questioned it.

Julie and Shively claimed cots beside James, shoulder to shoulder. Shively offered Julie the one with the fluffier pillow, she smirked but didn’t object.

Robert cycled through the room a few times.

First, he brought food, bowls of stew, still steaming. He didn’t say much but paused in front of Beverley a moment too long before placing her bowl down with more care than necessary.

The second time, he came to brief them.

“We set out at sunrise. We’ll need to move quickly and stay sharp.” His voice carried authority, not arrogance. Natural leadership. James listened, arms folded, eyes sharp. Two alphas in one room.

The third time, he just checked in. “You all doing alright?” His gaze again landed on Beverley, as if the question was mostly for her.

Finally, when the room had grown quiet and the night fully set in, he returned with a blanket over his shoulder and no armor on his chest. He stopped between the two cots, one belonging to Julie, the other to Beverley.

“Does it bother you if I sleep right here?” he asked, glancing toward Beverley with a softness in his tone that hadn’t been there earlier.

She looked up at him, not breaking eye contact. “No, of course not.”

He nodded and settled in, adjusting his blanket once before falling into stillness. His breathing slowed quickly, a man used to falling asleep wherever he needed.

Beverley lay frozen for a moment, unsure where to place her gaze. She glanced toward the ceiling, then to the wall, then… his face.

There was something about him in rest, unguarded and calm. She felt her breath hitch slightly, a whisper of something unspoken stirring deep in her chest.

She turned over, curling toward the wall, hiding her face in the crook of her elbow. 

If anyone were to make it through this journey unscathed, if fate were to spare just one person…

She hoped it would be him.

Beverley awoke before the others, the temple’s early routines still hardwired into her. As she shifted slightly, she realized her hand had slipped off the cot and was resting against Robert’s.

Fingers intertwined.

Her heart fluttered, and she felt a warm flush rise to her cheeks. Her hand began to sweat from the contact.

Robert stirred.

His eyes opened slowly, locking onto hers with quiet surprise. Then he noticed their hands. He pulled away gently but quickly, his voice low.

“I’m sorry. I’ve been told I move around a lot in my sleep… but handholding’s a first. I’m sorry.”

Beverley blinked, face calm despite the storm inside her. “It’s quite fine. I… I tend to do this in my sleep. In the temple, I’d often wake up holding someone’s hand. I’m sure I was the one who reached out.”

It was true. Many mornings at the temple had begun with the soft clasp of a fellow maiden’s hand. It was instinct to seek warmth and comfort.

Robert smiled, the kind of smile that came from genuine care. “Well, if it ever helps you feel safer or calmer… hold my hand anytime.”

His sincerity pierced the thin veil of stoicism she wore like armor. She didn’t respond, only nodded, unsure whether her voice would betray her.

Julie stirred next, yawning and stretching. “Coffee?” she mumbled.

James sat up, already brushing sand from his tunic. Shively, stubborn as ever, refused to move until Julie nudged him repeatedly.

“Jewels… you smell incredible,” he murmured groggily.

“That’s the coffee, you idiot,” she replied. “Which I will miss dearly.”

She gulped it down and tore into a strange, breaded dish with sweet fruit inside. “What is this?”

“Stonefruit kolach,” Robert answered from the doorway. “Common around here.”

James ran his fingers through his long dark hair, stubble now dotting his jawline. Despite the rough travel, he still had the look of a man in command.

“Robert,” he said, standing, “you know the path from here to Nirrowen?”

Robert nodded.

“Alright. Best course of action?”

Robert stepped forward and began with calm authority.

“From here to Nirrowen, we’re crossing a different kind of desert. You’ve had it easy so far with flat sands. But the Eastern stretch?”

He paused.

“It’s filled with Crawlers and Feeders. Fast, aggressive. You’ll need weapons. And unless you’ve fought them…”

“I have,” said James.

“So have I,” Julie echoed, their eyes meeting.

Robert nodded slowly. “Good. Then I only have to worry about half of you.”

Shively raised his hand lazily. “I’ve read about them… if that helps.”

Robert chuckled once, then turned serious. “We’ll need ammo. Supplies. And horses.”

James raised an eyebrow. “Do you have any?”

Robert replied, “I’ve got four trained mounts, used to harsh terrain. They’ll carry us to the edge of the Nirrowen cliffs. From there…”

He paused.

“You’ll see.”
