A messenger traveled between cities. He was unknown perhaps not even alive. Some swore he was a machine, others whispered he was a ghost. But all recognized him when he came. The prophecy had foretold that when the ancient city of Virellen began to stir, a summons would follow. And when it came, six would be chosen not to carry power, but to sacrifice part of themselves in order to save all of humanity. So, when word spread that a figure cloaked in black fabric streaked with rust and ash had been seen crossing the great desert that connected the five living cities, panic began to simmer beneath the surface of every wall and gate. He carried no banner. He answered no questions. 
The messenger arrived at dawn to the Eastern City, known to most as Etrien.
He rode silently through the dense canopy of moss-hung trees, branches parting for him as if they feared to brush against rust. Etrien, though the most fortified of the five living cities, was the most alive, its walls crawling with vines, its walkways shadowed by ancient leaves. The city relied on its camouflage as much as its weapons; the greenery concealed it from above, and from the ruins that watched from the horizon.
The figure passed through unchallenged. He reached the command ring just as the sun began to bleed through the canopy. The black-cloaked figure halted before the largest tent. Commander James Marric stepped out before the guards could react. 
Everyone in Etrien knew the stories. They were sung to children in their early training, songs about sacrifice, the City of Ash, and the six who would carry the world’s last breath. No one truly believed. And so when James saw the messenger standing outside his tent, the blood in his chest ran a little colder.
Without a word, the figure extended a single letter sealed in gray wax, stamped with the sigil of a flame burning inside an open eye. James took it. And the weight of it was immediate. Then, before his eyes, the messenger simply vanished, dissolved into the air like mist through sunlight, leaving only a faint scent of ozone and burning copper.
James tucked the letter into his breast pocket. He wouldn’t open it, not yet. Instead, he sat alone in his tent, surrounded by reports, maps, and the hum of morning drills outside. The usual noise of the Eastern City felt distant now, muffled beneath the weight pressing on his chest. He stared at the edges of old texts on the prophecy, verses passed down through fragmented oral tradition, diagrams inked by trembling hands long dead. He knew what this was. But he needed it to mean something more than just doom. 
Two hours passed. No answers came. Just silence. Finally, he pulled the envelope from his coat and studied the gray wax seal, now smudged slightly with the heat of his palm. It pulsed with a faint warmth. Or maybe that was just in his head. He broke the seal. As he slid the parchment out, the edge sliced clean across the side of his thumb. A thin ribbon of blood welled up instantly. James winced. “Now that’s not foreboding at all,” he muttered. “Real subtle.” He dabbed the cut on his sleeve, then turned his eyes to the contents of the letter.
There were no explanations. Just a list of six names written in a firm, slanted hand. He recognized a few immediately. One in particular. Julie Caelwyn. He leaned back in his chair, exhaling slowly. “Oh. Of course, it’s her.” James had known of Julie Caelwyn for years. Not personally, no one really knew Julie personally, but her reputation was legend. In every city between the Bastion and the Dust Reaches, her name carried weight. If you needed a job done, and done right, you called Julie. If you were lucky, she even answered. She was the sharpest blade on any battlefield, and twice as hard to hold. 
He spent the next hour packing his duffel bag, decisively, methodically. Years of military life had taught him how to fit his entire existence into one standard-issue sack, compartmentalized like his thoughts. Every strap cinched; every tool checked. By the time he tied it closed, he’d already run the mission scenario through his head a dozen times. Then came the harder part: leaving. James named a second-in-command for Etrien’s forces, temporarily, he told them. But he could feel it in the way the officers saluted, in the way the old guards clapped his shoulder, in the way the cook pressed an extra ration into his hands. These weren’t “see you later” goodbyes. They were funeral soft.
Still, he didn’t falter. Not when he stepped through the gate. Not when he crossed the shadowed tree line. Not when he began his descent through the southeastern gorge that led into the sand-choked borderlands between cities. Julie lived in the city of Kael’thera, just beyond the canyon rim.
If Etrien was spine and steel, Kael’thera was knives and whispers, a city carved into the cliffside, painted with dust and dusk light. It was half built, half broken, and wholly unpredictable. Trade flourished there, but so did theft, betrayal, and the kind of politics that made assassins look like diplomats. It relied on Etrien for protection. Even from itself. As James crossed into the city’s perimeter checkpoint, he felt it, the shift in air, in posture, in unspoken rules. The guards didn’t salute him here. They barely looked him in the eye.
Kael’thera was chaos wearing charm but Julie wasn’t hard to find. She never was. Not because she kept a schedule, but because people made space for her. The way dust cleared from a blade in motion. The way even the most reckless thugs in Kael’thera’s winding alleys suddenly found urgent business elsewhere when she passed. And to James’ slight irritation, she was also extraordinarily beautiful in that dangerous, unbothered kind of way that made people either want to fight her or follow her. Most chose wisely.
He caught up to her just outside a weapons stall, her arms crossed, hair pulled back, eyes sharp. “Julie,” he called out. “A moment, please.” She didn’t turn fully, just glanced over her shoulder. “What business do I have with you, and why should I listen?” she asked short, clean, efficient. James didn’t waste time. He held up the letter. “This is our business.” She saw the gray wax seal, her posture changing instantly. “…Well,” she said quietly. “We’ll need a private place to talk. Follow me.”
Her home was a small, stone-faced dwelling carved into the lower cliffside, far from the noise and stench of Kael’thera’s merchant tiers. Inside, it was as clean and precise as her reputation: a weapons rack by the door, maps on the walls, and a modest shelf of accolades and medals, most of which James recognized and a few he didn’t. She was more decorated than him. And James Marric had been hailed as a hero. He didn’t comment. He didn’t need to. Julie knew he’d seen them.
“So,” she said, pouring two glasses of something amber. “What other names are on the list?” James unfolded the letter and read quietly. When he reached “Shively Oren,” Julie’s head snapped up. 
“You’re joking.”
James raised an eyebrow. “You know him?” A flicker of something passed across her face. Excitement, maybe. Then something harder to read.
“We knew each other once,” she said, sitting straighter. “He’s not made for this. He jokes too much when he’s scared. Which is always.”
James folded the letter. “Then you’ll have to keep him alive.”
She looked at him.Then nodded once.
“Let’s discuss this before we get him. I’d like to have a firm understanding,” Julie said, folding her arms as she leaned back into her seat.

James nodded. “Fair.”

They unrolled the letter again, reading further into the fine-lined script beneath the names.

Six are named.
Six shall carry.
At the journey’s end, the vessel shall open and decide.

“A vessel?” Julie said, narrowing her eyes. “Did you receive the package yet?”

James shook his head. “No. I’m sure that’ll come when it’s needed.”

He hesitated, then added, “The letter was delivered by a… strange messenger. Not military. Didn’t speak. And when I took the letter, he disappeared. Just… dissolved. Right in front of me.”

Julie blinked. “Whoa. Like a ghost?”

James gave a short laugh. “I don’t believe in ghosts.”

“Well,” she muttered, “you might have to start.”

He tucked the letter away. “All I know is we have to get everyone on this list. Then I’m sure the vessel will come to us.”

Julie stood and stretched her arms behind her head. “Alright then, Mr. Marric.”

He raised an eyebrow at the formality.

“I’ll show you where we can find Shively.”

They arrived at the outskirts of Kael’thera’s outer edge, where the city lost its shape and bled into desert silence.

Fittingly, Shively Oren lived right at that edge, caught between civilization and solitude, more an observer of life than a participant. His home looked like it had been assembled from four different buildings and a ruined temple. Scrolls and wind chimes hung from the awning. A copper kettle was boiling over something that definitely wasn’t tea.

Before they could knock, the door creaked open.

“Ah, Julie,” Shively said, his mouth curling into a knowing grin. “I had a feeling I’d see you today.”

“It’s not for the best reason,” she said, pushing through his door without hesitation.

James followed.

Shively chuckled as he stepped aside. “Well, come in then. You always make yourself at home anyway.”

James didn’t say a word. He simply pulled the letter from his coat and unfolded it, letting the gray wax seal catch the light.

Shively’s smile faltered.

“What? Are you sure I’m on that list?” he asked, voice quieter now. “The prophecy speaks of heroes. I’m not one. I archive the heroes. I just keep the stories safe.”

Julie gave him a long blink, followed by a sigh. “You know the prophecy as well as I do. If you’re listed, you’re coming. Like it or not.”

Shively stared at the letter, then at them. He went still.

Then nodded, once. “Alright. You can count on me.”

He turned serious. “But be warned, I’m not much use when it comes to keeping anyone alive.”

His tone was sharp but honest.

Julie glanced at James, who said nothing.

Shively had been trained as a monk once—not the fighting kind. He could recite a hundred verses, unlock half the sacred vaults of Kael’thera, and catalog forgotten histories like they were grocery lists.

But when it came to blood and survival?

He had never pretended to be that kind of monk.

And they all knew it.

“I’m sure we’ll find you useful,” James said, offering the thought like a rope thrown across a widening gap. “You might even enjoy yourself if you start cataloging the journey.”

Shively tilted his head, considering. “Oh gods, I am going to take notes, aren’t I?”

James gave a rare, faint smile.

Then he glanced at Julie.

The way she looked at Shively… it was different.

Not pity. She didn’t see him as weak or burdensome. She looked at him like someone who had already decided to keep him safe, no matter the cost.

James didn’t know what to make of that. And frankly, it wasn’t his place to.

He cleared his throat. “You two gather your things. I’ll wait for you at the main trail, we need to move fast if we’re going to reach the next city before dusk tomorrow.”

Julie paused, halfway through checking the clip on her belt. “Who’s next?”

James opened the letter again and read aloud. “Someone by the name of Beverley Thorne. In Cindrelune.”

At that, Shively’s eyes lit up, like someone had just mentioned his favorite lost manuscript.

“A healer, I hope,” he said. “Cindrelune is the holy city.”

James nodded. “I hope so too.”

As the three set out, their boots kicked up soft plumes of sand beneath the low desert sun. The terrain was a slow stretch of dune mounds and broken clay, with the city of Kael’thera shrinking behind them like a mirage fading into memory.

There was little chatter at first.

Until Shively broke the silence.

“So,” he said, walking slightly sideways to face them, “if we’re the ones chosen to save the world, then surely I must have a very good reason to be here. I mean, I already know why Jewels would be picked" Julie rolled her eyes but didn’t interrupt “ and of course you too, High Commander Marric.”

James kept his eyes on the horizon, steady. “So you do know who I am.”

Shively shook his head. “Not exactly, Commander. I’ve never heard of you.”

James arched a brow. “Then how …”

“I’m a bit of a psychic,” Shively said brightly, like someone announcing they’d just remembered where they left their shoes. “It’s not terribly consistent, but I get... impressions. Moments of knowing things I shouldn’t. Claircognizance,technically. Though I don’t recommend trusting it during card games or life-threatening battles.”

James slowed for half a step, then looked at him sidelong. “You may be more useful than you think, Shively.”

Julie snorted. “Don’t encourage him.”

But James was already thinking it, how many stories in the old songs had a fool or a seer hiding their worth behind a crooked smile? He’d learned long ago that usefulness didn’t always look like discipline.

Chapter 2. 

The three  traveled for what felt like years to get through the gates of Cindrelune, the holy city that glowed with a constant beam of soft light rising into the sky, like a silent prayer cast upward. The streets were lined with smooth white stone and muted golden tapestries that shifted gently in the breeze. The air was still. Reverent and Sacred.

James exhaled, rubbing a hand over the back of his neck. He was tired, his steps a little heavier than before.

“I suggest we find Beverley,” he said, “and seek a few moments of rest.”

Julie gave a short nod. She didn’t feel tired, not yet. But she knew herself, she forgot to eat, to sleep, to breathe when on missions like this. And if this one truly was world-ending… she’d need her strength.

“Great idea,” Shively said, stretching his arms behind his head. “We’re going to need as much rest as we can get. That’s not a premonition, by the way, just a pattern I’ve noticed in all great stories: the deeper the plot, the less sleep the heroes get.”

James narrowed his eyes. “If we’ve been collecting prominent figures from each city, I’d wager Beverley’s someone important in this temple.”

They made their way to the largest of the ivory buildings, the central sanctuary.

Inside, cool air greeted them. The scent of sandalwood and spring water clung to the polished stone. Gentle music, almost like humming, echoed through unseen halls.

A soft voice met them as they stepped inside. “Welcome, visitors. How may we assist you?”

“We’re looking for someone,” James said. “Beverley Thorne.”

The voice grew warmer. “Ah. Then you’ve come to the right place.”

A young attendant, cloaked in soft blues and grays, disappeared behind a curtained archway. Within moments, she returned and guided them to a quiet office nestled deep in the temple.

Beverley was already waiting.

She sat with her hands folded in her lap, posture calm, eyes steady. She didn’t speak.

James stepped forward, not bothering to soften his tone. “You’re on the list.” He flashed the letter at her, pointing to her name. “We’ll rest tonight. We leave at first light.”

Shively stepped in quickly, voice lighter. “What he means to say is, we must prepare. And Commander Marric here has been operating on pure stubbornness for the past three days.”

Beverley gave a small nod, then stood. Without a word, she led them down a quiet corridor, where a set of guest chambers had already been prepared. They were given clean beds, food, water, and access to hot showers.

Julie and Shively claimed adjacent cots, their quiet banter echoing softly in the candlelit room. James took the one across from them, setting his bag against the wall.

He lied down, but he didn’t sleep. He listened to Shively’s muttering and to Julie’s short, exasperated responses. And to the silence of the fourth bed, still empty. Beverley hadn’t said a word since they'd arrived. And while she may have spoken with her presence, with her grace... To James, it felt like she was hiding something.

“James, who’s next to recover?” Julie asked, her voice low but firm, just needing the confirmation.

But when she looked over, James was already still, his head turned slightly toward the wall. His eyes were closed, breath even.

He must have fallen asleep listening to their banter.

“Jewels,” Shively began.

Julie’s head snapped toward him. “Shively, stop calling me that. You know I’ve never liked it.”

“I don’t call you that just because it’s a shortening of your name,” he said with a slight grin. “I call you that because you are exactly that. A jewel.”

Julie blinked, caught slightly off guard.

Then, without meaning to, she smirked. “Well… that’s a nice way to explain it.”

She leaned back into her cot, folding her arms behind her head. “Fine. But no one else can call me that. Just you.” Julie respected a man who didn’t cause problems, especially since she was always the one cleaning up the messes left by others. Shively? He admired beautiful things.  Relics, language, old paintings, lost cities.

But he didn’t see Julie as an object.

He saw her like a prized artifact, not to possess, but to protect. A piece of living history that had survived everything and still shined. The two fell asleep mid-conversation, their banter fading into quiet breaths.

Later, long after the lights had dimmed and the halls of the temple had fallen into silence, Beverley entered the room.
She moved softly, barefoot, the hem of her linen robes whispering against the floor. She stepped inside just to check on them, but paused when she heard Shively sleep-talking.
“Robert… you need to tell Beverley…” he murmured. “She only really talks to you… why does she only talk to you?”
Beverley froze, hand still on the doorframe.
Her heart beat once, then again, heavier.
Robert.
The name settled over her like a chill. She hadn’t heard it aloud in years, not like that. And if Shively had seen him in some kind of dream, was Robert the next one on the list?
The thought made her stomach turn. It would break her heart to know he’d be dragged into this. Into danger. Into this… end.
It was a temple guard who had first spoken the prophecy aloud.
It had come when the skies began to change, when the clouds turned black and rain no longer fell as water, but as rust and ash. 
It just… covered everything. Like the world itself was mourning.
That was when the whispers began, when ancient writings resurfaced from long-lost vaults. The internal death of the world had begun. Not from war, or famine, but from disconnection. From allowing technology to overtake spirit. From building machines to do what only the soul was meant to feel.
Beverley had spent her youth studying these writings.
She knew what the prophecy meant:
The earth had collapsed under the weight of its own abandonment.
The skies wept not blood but memory.
And the only way to birth something new… was to return the Pulse.
A state of being the world had long forgotten.
When dawn broke, Beverley stood silently at the temple gates, her bag already packed. She had slept a little, just enough to replenish her body, if not her heart. Her sandals were laced tight, her robes layered for travel. She knew what lay ahead might take her life.
Might take all of their lives.
She had said her goodbyes with no words, only small gestures, soft touches to shoulders, lingering looks across sacred halls. Her fellow temple dwellers didn’t ask questions. They knew.
And so did she.
She shed no tears.
James was the first to rise. The scent of baked bread and citrus tea lifted him from sleep with a strange optimism. He hadn’t expected a temple to provide such a full breakfast, yet here it was, a small feast to send them off.
Julie and Shively, however, needed more coaxing.
The smell of strong, dark coffee did most of the work. Beverley returned just as they were finishing, moving quietly with the grace of one used to discipline and silence. She nodded once toward them, and they understood it was time.
