They rode hard across the last stretch of desert until the sand abruptly gave way to stone. The land simply ended, an immense cliff face, jagged and wide, dropping down into something none of them expected.

A jungle.

Dense vines and towering trees rose out of the canyon floor, a wild tapestry of greens shimmering with mist. The sound of unseen creatures echoed up through the chasm. And laced among the foliage, like bones in a graveyard, were enormous metal husks. There lied the broken wings of aircraft, rusted engines split open, and the remains of machines long abandoned.

James leaned forward in his saddle, squinting down with a stunned whisper. “Wow…” He had never been here, but he knew the stories. Nirrowen. The land condemned. The city of sinners blamed for the end of the old world, for the skies raining rust and ash.

Robert gestured with one hand, introducing it as though unveiling a painting. “Everyone… this is Nirrowen. The jungle in the desert. The land where life and machine chose to grow together.”

Beverley’s sharp eyes swept over everything. The grass climbing the stone, the vines choking the wreckage, the horses shifting uneasily at the edge of the drop. She frowned.

Julie was looking down as well, but her concern was elsewhere. “Who are we even picking up here? And why do we need someone from the city that supposedly doomed us all?”

James pulled the letter from his coat and read aloud. “His name is Frank. If the pattern holds, he’s either someone in high rank, or someone far more important than he looks.”

Shively leaned on the horn of his saddle, tilting his head as though listening to something only he could hear. Robert’s eyes flicked toward Beverley, catching the faint crease of worry on her brow. “What’s wrong?” She didn’t look at him just at the horses, restless and stamping. “The descent,” she said softly. “They won’t make it.”

Robert lifted Beverley’s concern with a steady nod. “This is where we carry our own weight. Gather your gear. These horses go no further.”

The group hesitated for a moment, but then began unstrapping bags and supplies. Sand shifted beneath them, the cliff edge humming faintly as if reminding them of the risk ahead.

James paused, glancing back at the restless mounts. “The horses… are they base-trained?”

Robert smiled pleasantly, resting a hand on one animal’s neck. “Yes. I’ve been down this road before. They know what to do if one of us doesn’t make it back. They’ll dart for the nearest outpost. They race days of land in hours. They’re faster without cargo.”

It was practical, but oddly reassuring.

Meanwhile, Fang stuck so close to Beverley that his flank brushed her knee as she adjusted her pack. The great hybrid beast even clamped his jaws gently onto the strap of her satchel, carrying it so she didn’t have to. She gave him a quiet pat in thanks, though her eyes stayed fixed on the long drop ahead.

Robert slung his bag low, securing the straps around his boots. “Alright, everyone. After me.”

Then, without another word, he stepped to the edge and dropped.

He landed on the angled slope of sand below, knees bending into a practiced slide. His pack acted as ballast, riding just ahead of his boots like a board. The natural curve of the land caught him, slowing his descent until he vanished into the mist that clung to the jungle canopy.

James exhaled once, set his jaw, and followed. His heavier frame kicked up more grit, but he stayed balanced, his body angled sharp and sure.

The others exchanged glances, then one by one hurled themselves after.

Julie whooped mid-slide, Shively shouted something about “momentum being metaphorical,” Beverley went quietly but firm, Fang bounding down beside her like he’d done this all his life.

By the time they reached the bottom, their boots sank into damp earth, heavy with moss and the scent of green life.

For the first time since the journey began, the air was not dry and bitter. It was wet.

And ahead of them buried in vines, steel ribs, and half-swallowed towers was Nirrowen.

Robert raised a hand to the others. “Stay here. Too many of us will draw eyes.”

James gave a short nod, understanding.

Robert slipped through the curtain of vines, boots sinking into damp soil. There were no gates and no walls. Just homes fashioned from rusted machine parts and living greenery, old engines hollowed into shelters, and broad jungle leaves bent into water collectors that dripped steadily into clay jars. Nirrowen lived by fusing what remained of the world. Metal and vine, ruin and life.

He didn’t make it far before three men stepped into his path. They weren’t rugged soldiers. Their frames were thin, their eyes sharp with mistrust.

Robert stopped. “I’m looking for a lad named Frank. Point me to him.”

The youngest, barely twenty, narrowed his eyes. “Frankie? What do you want with him?”

“He’s part of the prophecy,” Robert answered simply. “His name is on the list. He comes with me.”

One of the older men scoffed. “Prophecy? You bringin’ religion in here, spouting doom and saviors? You’ve come to the wrong place. Frankie’s not part of no prophecy.”

The third man crossed his arms. “Trying to save the earth, are you? Well, we won’t be any part of it.”

Before Robert could answer, Beverley slipped silently from the vines behind him and placed a hand on his shoulder, easing him back a half step.

Her voice was gentle but firm, carrying an undeniable weight. “Please. We cannot continue without him. I know you’re afraid, so are we. But this is not the time to hide behind anger. Just let us speak with Frank, and we’ll be gone.”

The men looked at her, suspicion cracking under the force of her presence. Whether it was the clarity of her words or simply the calm conviction in her eyes, they shifted uneasily, then nodded.

“Alright,” the youngest muttered. “Wait here.” He turned and disappeared between the shanties.

Robert stood in silence, his jaw set. His heart, however, beat a fraction faster. He didn’t know if it was the urgency that moved the men, or Beverley herself.

The men returned, and with them came a boy. He looked no more than sixteen. A young boy who was thin, sharp-eyed, with a posture that carried more confidence than his years should allow.

Robert’s brow furrowed. “This is Frank?”

He took a step forward, ready to question, but Beverley’s hand found his arm again, her touch was a wordless command to wait.

The boy straightened his shoulders. “I’m Frank. Frank O’Connel, named after my father, the great Frank O’Connel.”

Robert cleared his throat, adjusting his stance, trying to temper his skepticism with politeness. “My apologies, son. I assumed we were meant to gather your father, not you.”

Frankie smiled faintly, but it was a smile edged with something harder. Defiance, maybe. “I’m pretty sure you came here for me.”

Robert’s eyes narrowed. “What makes you think that?”

The boy’s expression sobered. “For one… my dad’s been dead for ten years. You don’t need a dead man for your prophecy.”

A hush fell over the group. Even the jungle seemed to still, the hum of insects fading beneath the weight of his words.
